# 1. THE STRATEGIC PURPOSE

My Impact Director is a social enterprise initiative developed by My Social Impact.

Our vision:

**Imagine a world where social impact was taken as seriously as financial performance.**

The first job of My Impact Director is to review an organisation's impact report.

We have developed two complementary reviews:

### 1. Social Impact Claims Code

**Can we trust what the report claims?**

Examines the evidence, proportion, transparency, balance and learning behind social impact claims.

### 2. Social Impact Excellence

**What does the report demonstrate about how social impact is managed?**

Examines Purpose, Leadership, Data, Delivery and Communications.

### 3. Impact Report Excellence — NEW

**How effectively does the report communicate its impact?**

Examines Clarity, Storytelling, Design, Engagement and Communication.

This third assessment is fundamentally about the quality of the report as a piece of communication.

It brings a creative and editorial perspective to impact reporting.

A report can contain excellent evidence but communicate it badly.

Equally, a visually beautiful report can contain weak or misleading claims.

The three assessments must remain distinct.

Together, they provide a much more complete understanding of an impact report.

# 2. THE FIVE PILLARS OF IMPACT REPORT EXCELLENCE

Create a structured professional assessment based on five equally weighted pillars:

1. **Clarity**
2. **Storytelling**
3. **Design**
4. **Engagement**
5. **Communication**

Each pillar contains five assessment criteria.

There are therefore 25 criteria in total.

Each criterion is scored from 0 to 4.

Each pillar is worth 20 points.

The total possible score is 100.

Implement this as:

**Impact Report Excellence Methodology — Version 1.0**

It must be documented, version-controlled, testable and capable of refinement through future professional calibration.

The methodology is developed by My Social Impact as a new report-review framework.

Do not present it as an externally validated standard or existing independent accreditation.

# 3. PILLAR ONE — CLARITY

## Central question

**Is the report easy to read, navigate and understand?**

A successful impact report should make complicated information accessible without sacrificing accuracy.

We are assessing the reader's ability to understand the content with minimal unnecessary effort.

### Criterion C1 — Plain English

Assess whether the language is clear, direct and understandable.

Consider:

- Sentence length and complexity.
- Use of jargon and unexplained acronyms.
- Unnecessary technical terminology.
- Ambiguous statements.
- Whether complex ideas are explained in accessible language.

High scores require writing that communicates effectively without oversimplifying important ideas.

### Criterion C2 — Structure and Organisation

Assess whether the report follows a logical and understandable structure.

Consider:

- Opening introduction.
- Logical progression of sections.
- Clear grouping of related information.
- Appropriate use of summaries.
- Whether readers can understand how the report fits together.

### Criterion C3 — Navigation

Assess how easily readers can find information.

Consider:

- Contents pages where appropriate.
- Clear headings.
- Useful section titles.
- Page numbering.
- Cross-references.
- Ease of locating important findings.

A short report should not be penalised for omitting a contents page that it does not need.

### Criterion C4 — Information Hierarchy

Assess whether the most important information is sufficiently prominent.

Consider:

- Headline findings.
- Summaries.
- Supporting information.
- Appropriate emphasis.
- Whether important messages become buried in detail.

### Criterion C5 — Cognitive Readability

Assess how much effort the report demands from readers.

Consider:

- Dense blocks of text.
- Paragraph structure.
- Avoidable repetition.
- Explanation of unfamiliar concepts.
- Appropriate information density.

Do not automatically equate short sentences or limited content with good readability.

**A clear report makes complex information easier to understand, not merely shorter.**

# 4. PILLAR TWO — STORYTELLING

## Central question

**Does the report tell a compelling and coherent story about the difference the organisation makes?**

Impact reporting is fundamentally about change.

The strongest reports connect organisational purpose, action, evidence and human experience into a meaningful narrative.

### Criterion S1 — Narrative Structure

Assess whether the report has a coherent narrative.

Consider:

- Where the organisation started.
- What it intended to achieve.
- What it did.
- What changed.
- What happens next.

The report should have an understandable progression rather than simply presenting disconnected information.

### Criterion S2 — Human Stories

Assess whether the report brings the impact to life through relevant experiences and perspectives.

Consider:

- Beneficiary experiences.
- Stakeholder voices.
- Real-world examples.
- Quotes.
- Individual or community perspectives.

Do not automatically penalise reports where individual stories would be inappropriate, unsafe or irrelevant.

Avoid rewarding emotional manipulation.

### Criterion S3 — Meaningful Case Studies

Assess whether case studies and examples help readers understand the impact being described.

Consider:

- Relevance.
- Specificity.
- Context.
- Connection to organisational objectives.
- Integration with wider findings.

Case studies should illuminate the report rather than merely decorate it.

### Criterion S4 — Emotional Connection

Assess whether the report gives readers a meaningful reason to care.

Consider:

- Authenticity.
- Empathy.
- Human relevance.
- Tone.
- Connection between evidence and lived experience.

Emotional engagement must never come at the expense of accuracy, dignity or ethical storytelling.

### Criterion S5 — Narrative Coherence

Assess whether the different stories, statistics, examples and sections reinforce a consistent central narrative.

Consider whether readers finish with a clear understanding of the organisation's purpose, activities and contribution.

The overall story should be memorable without exaggeration.

# 5. PILLAR THREE — DESIGN

## Central question

**Is the report professionally and creatively designed to support understanding?**

This pillar examines visual communication, layout and art direction.

**It cannot be assessed properly using extracted PDF text alone.**

The assessment engine must inspect the actual visual appearance of the document.

### Criterion D1 — Typography

Assess:

- Typeface choices.
- Font hierarchy.
- Text size.
- Line spacing.
- Legibility.
- Consistency.
- Relationship between headings and body text.

Typography should contribute to readability and identity.

### Criterion D2 — Layout and Composition

Assess:

- Page composition.
- Margins.
- Alignment.
- Whitespace.
- Balance.
- Layout consistency.
- Density of information.

Whitespace is valuable when used intentionally, not automatically.

### Criterion D3 — Colour and Visual Identity

Assess:

- Colour choices.
- Brand consistency.
- Contrast.
- Visual coherence.
- Appropriateness for the audience.
- Whether colour contributes meaningfully to communication.

A restrained black-and-white report can score highly if the design is appropriate and effective.

### Criterion D4 — Photography, Illustration and Art Direction

Assess the quality and purpose of visual content.

Consider:

- Photography quality.
- Relevance.
- Selection and cropping.
- Captions.
- Illustration quality.
- Visual consistency.
- How effectively images support the story.
- Ethical and dignified representation.

Do not reward stock photography simply because it is visually attractive.

Do not penalise a report for having few photographs where that is a deliberate and appropriate choice.

### Criterion D5 — Overall Design Quality

Assess the report as a complete designed publication.

Consider:

- Consistency.
- Professional finish.
- Originality where appropriate.
- Visual coherence.
- Relationship between content and design.
- Quality of execution.

A strong report should feel intentionally designed, not mechanically assembled.

# 6. PILLAR FOUR — ENGAGEMENT

## Central question

**Does the report capture attention and encourage people to explore its content?**

This pillar focuses on audience engagement and the effectiveness of the overall reading experience.

It is distinct from Design, which evaluates visual execution.

### Criterion E1 — First Impression

Assess the effectiveness of the opening experience.

Consider:

- Cover.
- Opening statement.
- Introduction.
- Immediate sense of purpose.
- Whether readers are given a compelling reason to continue.

### Criterion E2 — Pacing and Variety

Assess whether the report sustains interest through appropriate variation.

Consider:

- Changes in content rhythm.
- Balance between text and visuals.
- Case studies.
- Data.
- Pull quotes.
- Section breaks.
- Appropriate alternation between detailed and concise material.

Avoid rewarding variety for its own sake.

### Criterion E3 — Visual Engagement

Assess whether imagery and visual storytelling draw readers into the subject.

Consider:

- Strong visual moments.
- Illustrations.
- Photography.
- Infographics.
- Visual storytelling.
- Integration of graphics with content.

The focus is on the contribution of visuals to reader engagement, not merely their technical design quality.

### Criterion E4 — Audience Connection

Assess how effectively the report addresses its intended readers.

Potential audiences include:

- Funders.
- Donors.
- Trustees.
- Staff.
- Beneficiaries.
- Investors.
- Partners.
- The wider public.

The report should consider what its audiences need to know and why it matters.

Do not assume every report must address every audience.

### Criterion E5 — Sustained Interest and Participation

Assess whether the report encourages readers to stay engaged and explore further.

Consider:

- Thought-provoking questions.
- Relevant links.
- Opportunities to explore detailed evidence.
- Useful signposting.
- Appropriate calls to action.
- Digital engagement where applicable.

Do not penalise a static PDF simply because it does not have interactive features.

The assessment must be proportionate to the intended medium.

# 7. PILLAR FIVE — COMMUNICATION

## Central question

**Does the report communicate its most important messages effectively and memorably?**

This final pillar evaluates the success of the report as a communications product.

It is distinct from Clarity, which focuses on ease of understanding, and Storytelling, which focuses on narrative.

### Criterion M1 — Key Messages

Assess whether the report communicates its most important conclusions clearly.

Consider:

- Central message.
- Key achievements.
- Significant findings.
- Main challenges.
- Priorities.
- Takeaways.

Readers should not need to search through the document to discover what matters most.

### Criterion M2 — Data Communication

Assess how effectively numerical information is communicated.

Consider:

- Charts.
- Tables.
- Infographics.
- Explanatory captions.
- Appropriate graphical choices.
- Meaningful comparisons.
- Clear labels.
- Presentation of uncertainty.

Do not reward decorative graphs that make the data harder to understand.

Do not duplicate the Claims Code's evidential validity assessment.

Here we are evaluating **how effectively the data is communicated**, not whether the underlying evidence is valid.

### Criterion M3 — Tone and Voice

Assess whether the report uses an appropriate, credible and consistent tone.

Consider:

- Audience appropriateness.
- Confidence.
- Authenticity.
- Consistency.
- Respect.
- Avoidance of excessive promotional language.

Tone should suit the organisation and its subject matter.

### Criterion M4 — Conclusions and Takeaways

Assess whether readers finish with a clear understanding of:

- What was achieved.
- What was learned.
- What remains challenging.
- What the organisation intends to do next.

The report should provide a meaningful conclusion rather than simply stop.

### Criterion M5 — Communication Effectiveness

Assess whether the whole report succeeds in delivering its intended messages.

Consider:

- Memorable communication.
- Accessibility of conclusions.
- Alignment with audience needs.
- Effectiveness of supporting explanations.
- Suitability for the chosen publication medium.

Judge the report on how effectively it communicates, rather than simply how attractive it looks.

# 8. SCORING METHODOLOGY

Each of the 25 criteria is scored from 0 to 4.

| Score | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| 0 | Not demonstrated or seriously ineffective |
| 1 | Weak — substantial improvements required |
| 2 | Developing — partially effective, with clear shortcomings |
| 3 | Good — effective, with identifiable opportunities to improve |
| 4 | Excellent — highly effective, thoughtfully executed and appropriate to purpose |

Each criterion contributes a maximum of four points.

Five criteria per pillar produce a maximum of 20.

Five pillars produce a maximum of 100.

### Calculation

For a fully assessable report:

- Clarity: 0–20
- Storytelling: 0–20
- Design: 0–20
- Engagement: 0–20
- Communication: 0–20

**Impact Report Excellence Score = sum of all five pillar scores.**

Maximum: 100.

This deliberately matches the 0–100 scale of the other My Impact Director assessments.

## Assessment safeguards

### Proportionality

Do not automatically favour expensive, elaborate reports over simple publications.

A modest, well-written and thoughtfully designed report can score highly.

### Purpose and audience

Judge the effectiveness of the report relative to its intended purpose and audience.

### Ethical representation

Do not reward sensationalism, exploitative photography or manipulative storytelling.

### Evidence-based observations

Every important finding should cite relevant examples or pages.

### No invented design observations

Do not claim that a report has poor typography, weak photography or ineffective layouts unless those elements have genuinely been inspected.

### Not assessable

If a criterion cannot reasonably be evaluated, mark it not assessable.

Do not silently give a zero.

If visual document inspection is unavailable, do not issue a definitive Impact Report Excellence score.

When a genuinely inapplicable criterion is excluded, transparently normalise that pillar's assessed criteria to 20 points and disclose coverage.

Require at least four assessable criteria in each pillar and 22 assessable criteria overall before publishing a definitive score.

Otherwise, provide an explicitly provisional qualitative review.

### Consistency

Use the same scoring definitions across reports.

Store the methodology version used.

Allow future calibration against human professional reviewers.

# 10. RECOMMENDATIONS MUST BE PRACTICAL

The third assessment should go well beyond saying that a report looks good or bad.

It should identify specific opportunities for improvement.

For example:

**Weak opening**

"The report begins with three pages of organisational background before explaining what changed during the year."

Recommendation:

"Lead with a concise summary of the year's most important outcomes, then provide organisational background."

**Dense typography**

"Pages 12–15 contain long text blocks with limited visual hierarchy."

Recommendation:

"Introduce stronger subheadings, break the content into shorter sections and highlight key findings."

**Weak data communication**

"An important table contains useful information but does not explain the main conclusion readers should draw."

Recommendation:

"Introduce a short takeaway above the table and improve the labels and explanatory text."

**Disconnected storytelling**

"The report includes personal stories but does not connect them to the wider outcomes."

Recommendation:

"Introduce each case study in the context of the relevant programme objective and explain what it demonstrates."

Each recommendation should contain:

1. The issue.
2. Why it matters.
3. The relevant evidence or page.
4. A practical suggested improvement.
5. Priority or likely benefit.

Where appropriate, offer specific rewriting suggestions or alternative communication approaches.

Avoid vague generic advice such as "improve storytelling" or "use more engaging graphics".
